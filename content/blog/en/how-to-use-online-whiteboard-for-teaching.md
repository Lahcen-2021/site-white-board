A classroom whiteboard has one big strength: students watch the explanation being built, line by line. An online whiteboard keeps that strength and fixes the annoying parts. You never run out of space, you never have to wipe away the example you wanted to come back to, and at the end of the lesson the whole board can go home with the students as a picture.

This guide walks through how to teach with one, from preparing the board to sharing it afterwards. The examples use SiteWhiteboard, which is free and opens in the browser, but most of the advice works with any whiteboard tool.

## What you need

Very little. A computer, tablet or phone with a modern browser is enough to draw. In a classroom, connect the computer to a projector, a TV or an interactive display. For a remote lesson, share your screen in Zoom, Google Meet, Microsoft Teams or whatever your school uses.

If you can, use a tablet with a stylus or a laptop with a touch screen. Writing equations and labels with a mouse works, but handwriting with a pen feels much closer to a real marker. A cheap drawing tablet plugged into a laptop is another good option.

## Before class: prepare the board

Open a new board a few minutes before the lesson and set up the parts you do not want to draw live.

- **Insert the material.** Paste a diagram, a photo of a textbook page or a worksheet with Ctrl+V, or drag the image file onto the board. You can then write on top of it.
- **Place the lesson title and goals** with the text tool (shortcut T) in the top corner, so students know where the lesson is going.
- **Leave empty space.** The board is endless, so spread the sections out. One area for the explanation, one for examples, one for questions.
- **Prepare sticky notes** for key words or questions you will reveal later. Double-tap an empty spot to create one.

If you teach the same lesson to several groups, save the prepared board as a project file from the boards menu. Next time, open a fresh board and insert that file with the Insert button. You start every class from the same clean template.

## During class: explain step by step

The point of a whiteboard is that students see your thinking in order. A few habits make that work better on a screen.

**Write bigger than you think.** Text that looks fine on your laptop can be unreadable at the back of a room. Zoom in on the part you are working on (pinch with two fingers or use the mouse wheel) and write there. Zoom out when you want to show the whole picture.

**Use colour with a purpose.** Pick one colour for the main explanation, a second for corrections or warnings and a third for the answer. Students quickly learn that red means "watch out here." Six marker colours are one tap away, and the + button opens more.

**Draw diagrams with shapes and arrows.** Rectangles, ellipses, triangles and diamonds are in the shape tool (S). Arrows (L) stay attached to shapes when you move them, so you can rearrange a diagram while you talk without redrawing it.

**Undo freely.** A wrong stroke is gone with Ctrl+Z, or the undo button at the bottom right on a touch screen. This makes students less nervous when they come up to write, too.

**Move, don't erase.** On a physical board you erase old work to make room. Online, drag the view to a fresh area with the hand tool (H) or by holding the space bar. The earlier work stays there if someone asks about it.

## Get students involved

A whiteboard lesson goes better when students do some of the drawing.

- Ask a student to come to the front and solve the next step on the interactive display or tablet.
- In a remote class, ask students to describe what you should draw. "Where does this arrow go?" turns watching into thinking.
- Put each student question on a sticky note during the lesson and answer them together at the end.
- For revision, show a diagram with the labels missing and let the class fill them in.

SiteWhiteboard boards live on one device and there is no live multi-user editing. In practice this is fine for most lessons: the teacher draws, and the class watches the projector or the shared screen. If you need several students drawing on the same board at the same time from their own devices, use a collaborative tool for that activity.

## After class: share the board

This is where an online board clearly beats the classroom wall. When the lesson ends, click export and save the board as a PNG image. You can then:

- post it in Google Classroom, Moodle, Teams or your class group chat
- add it to the lesson slides for next year
- print it for students who were absent

Because each board is saved in your browser automatically, you can also keep the board and continue it in the next lesson. Close the tab and the board is still there when you come back on the same device and browser.

## A simple lesson structure that works

If you want a starting point, try this layout for a 45 minute lesson:

1. **Top left:** title, date and two learning goals.
2. **Left column:** warm-up question on a sticky note. Students answer out loud while you write their ideas.
3. **Centre:** the main explanation, built step by step with one colour, with key words in a second colour.
4. **Right side:** two worked examples, then one example students solve with you.
5. **Bottom:** questions on sticky notes and a short summary in your own words.

Export the board at the end and the summary sheet is ready.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Too much on screen at once.** Zoom in on the part you are explaining. A full board on a projector is hard to read.
- **Thin lines.** Pick the middle or thick size for anything students must read from a distance.
- **Forgetting the backup.** Boards are stored in the browser on that one device. If the school computer clears its data every night, export the board before you leave.
- **Never showing the whole board.** At the end, zoom out so the class sees how all the parts connect. Use the fit button to frame everything at once.

## Try it in your next lesson

You do not need to change your whole teaching style. Start with one lesson where you would normally use the classroom board, open a board on the projector and teach exactly as you usually do. Most teachers find that the exported picture at the end is enough reason to keep going.

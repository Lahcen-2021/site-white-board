"Digital whiteboard" is used for several different things, which makes the term confusing. Sometimes it means a large touch screen on a classroom wall. Sometimes it means an app on a tablet. Sometimes it means a website where you draw in the browser. They all do the same basic job: give you a surface on a screen where you can write, draw and organise ideas the way you would on a real whiteboard.

This guide explains the different types, how they work and how to choose one.

## The three types of digital whiteboard

### 1. Interactive displays

These are the big screens you see in classrooms and meeting rooms. Older models were projectors pointed at a touch-sensitive board. Newer ones are large touch screens, often 65 to 86 inches, with a computer built in or connected.

You write on them with a finger or a special pen. They are expensive hardware, and they still need software to draw with. That software is usually one of the next two types.

### 2. Whiteboard apps

These are programs you install on a computer, tablet or phone. Microsoft Whiteboard on Windows is a common example. Apps can work offline and use the device's pen support well. The downside is installation and updates, and sometimes they only work on one operating system.

### 3. Online whiteboards

An online whiteboard runs in a web browser. You open a page and start drawing. There is nothing to install, and it works the same on Windows, Mac, Chromebook, Android, iPhone and iPad. SiteWhiteboard is an online whiteboard, as are Miro, Excalidraw and many others.

Online whiteboards are the most flexible type. You can use one on a laptop, on a tablet, or on an interactive display by opening the same website in its browser.

## How an online whiteboard works

Under the hood, an online whiteboard is a web page with a drawing area, usually an HTML canvas. When you move your finger or mouse, the page records the points and draws a smooth line through them. Shapes, sticky notes and text are stored as objects, which is why you can select them, move them and change their colour after drawing.

Most online boards are "infinite": there is no edge. You move around by dragging the view and zoom in and out with a pinch or the mouse wheel.

Where the board is saved depends on the tool:

- **In the cloud.** Collaborative tools like Miro save boards on their servers. This lets several people edit together and lets you open the board on any device, but you need an account.
- **In your browser.** Tools like SiteWhiteboard save the board in the browser's local storage on your device. You need no account and nothing leaves your device, but the board is only on that device unless you export it.

## What you can do on a digital whiteboard

The basic tools are similar across most products:

- **Pen or marker** for freehand drawing and handwriting, usually in several colours and thicknesses
- **Eraser** to remove strokes
- **Sticky notes** for short ideas that you can move around
- **Shapes** such as rectangles, circles, triangles and diamonds
- **Arrows and lines** to connect ideas, ideally staying attached when you move shapes
- **Text** for clean typed labels
- **Images** you can paste or drop onto the board and write on
- **Export** to save the board as an image or a PDF

On top of these, collaborative platforms add live cursors, comments, templates and integrations with other work software.

## Who uses digital whiteboards

**Teachers** use them to explain lessons on a projector or in online classes, and send the finished board to students afterwards. See [how to use an online whiteboard for teaching](/blog/how-to-use-online-whiteboard-for-teaching/).

**Students** use them to revise, make mind maps, work through problems and plan essays. A board is a big piece of paper that never runs out.

**Tutors** use them in one-to-one lessons, often on a tablet with a pen, sharing the screen in a video call.

**Teams at work** use them for brainstorming, planning, retrospectives and drawing processes or system diagrams during meetings.

**Kids** use them to draw. A simple board with big colours and finger drawing is a good screen activity that is creative rather than passive.

## Digital vs physical whiteboard

A physical whiteboard is still great for a team in one room. It is instant, everyone can grab a marker and nobody needs a device. But it has limits that a digital board does not:

- the space runs out
- you have to erase to continue
- the work is lost unless someone takes a photo
- people in other places cannot see it

A digital whiteboard fixes all four. The trade-off is that you need a screen, and drawing with a mouse is less natural than with a marker. A tablet with a pen closes most of that gap.

## How to choose one

Start with how you will use it:

- **Drawing alone or showing your screen?** Choose a fast online whiteboard with no sign-up.
- **Several people editing together?** Choose a collaborative tool with accounts.
- **A classroom with an interactive display?** Any online whiteboard will run in the display's browser, so choose the one that is simplest for you and your students.

The easiest way to understand a digital whiteboard is to try one. Open a board, draw a few lines, add a sticky note and export the picture. It takes less than a minute.

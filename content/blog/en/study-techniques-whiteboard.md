Rereading notes feels like studying, but it is one of the weakest ways to learn. You recognise the material, so it feels familiar, and familiar feels like knowing. Then the exam asks you to produce the answer from memory and it is not there.

A whiteboard pushes you into the methods that actually work, because a blank board asks you to produce something. Here are seven techniques, why they work, and how to use each one on an online whiteboard.

## Why a whiteboard helps you study

Learning research keeps pointing to the same few ideas. Pulling information out of your memory (retrieval practice) strengthens it more than putting it in again. Spreading study over several days (spacing) beats cramming. Explaining a topic in your own words exposes gaps.

A whiteboard is good for all three. It is large, so you can lay out a whole topic. It is quick to clear or move, so you can test yourself again and again. And it is visual, so you see how ideas connect. An online whiteboard adds unlimited space and the ability to save each session.

## 1. Blurting

Pick a topic. Close your notes. Write everything you remember about it on the board, as fast as you can, for ten minutes. Then open your notes and add what you missed in a different colour.

The second colour is the important part. It shows you exactly what you do not know yet. Next time, start with those gaps.

On SiteWhiteboard, use black or blue for the blurt and red for the corrections. Export the board as a PNG so you can compare it with your next attempt.

## 2. Active recall with sticky notes

Write a question on the front of a sticky note, such as "What are the three stages of mitosis?" Put a set of these questions on one side of the board. Answer each one out loud or on the board next to it, then check your notes.

Move notes you answered correctly to the right side, and leave the ones you got wrong on the left. Next session, start with the left side. This is a simple version of flashcards with a visible pile of "still to learn."

## 3. The Feynman technique

Named after the physicist Richard Feynman, this method has four steps:

1. Write the name of a concept at the top of the board.
2. Explain it underneath in simple words, as if teaching a 12-year-old. Draw diagrams.
3. Where you get stuck or start using jargon, you have found a gap. Go back to your notes.
4. Simplify your explanation and try again.

A whiteboard suits this method because teaching is naturally done at a board. Some students record themselves explaining, which makes the gaps even more obvious.

## 4. Mind maps

Put the main topic in the centre and draw branches for the subtopics, then smaller branches for details. Use a colour for each branch.

Mind maps are best for subjects with lots of connected ideas, like history, biology and literature. Draw one from memory first, then fill it in from your notes. That way it doubles as retrieval practice. We have a full guide on [how to make a mind map online](/blog/how-to-make-a-mind-map-online/).

## 5. Worked problems, one step per line

For maths, physics, chemistry and economics, reading worked examples is not enough. Cover the solution, write the problem on the board and solve it step by step yourself. Only then compare with the answer.

Keep every attempt on the board, including the wrong ones. Circle mistakes in red and write what went wrong next to them. A board full of corrected mistakes is a personal list of traps to avoid in the exam.

## 6. Spaced repetition with saved boards

Spacing means reviewing a topic several times with growing gaps: after one day, then three days, then a week. You can track this on the board itself. Write the topics in a list on one side, with the dates you reviewed each one.

## 7. Timelines and process diagrams

Some topics are a sequence: historical events, a chemical process, the steps of an algorithm, the plot of a novel. Draw a long line across the board and place each event or step on it with a sticky note. Add arrows for causes and effects.

Then cover parts of it and rebuild them from memory. Being able to reproduce a sequence from scratch is a strong sign you know it.

## A study session that combines them

Here is a one-hour session for one topic:

- **10 minutes:** blurt everything you know.
- **10 minutes:** check your notes and add missing points in red.
- **20 minutes:** explain the hardest part with the Feynman technique, or solve three practice problems.
- **10 minutes:** turn your questions into sticky notes for active recall next time.
- **10 minutes:** draw a small mind map of the topic from memory.

Export the board at the end. Next time you study the topic, start a fresh board and blurt again before looking at the old one.

## Tips

- Study on a tablet with a pen if you can. Handwriting helps memory more than typing for many people.
- Keep each board to one topic. Smaller boards are easier to review.
- Do not make your board pretty. A messy board you made from memory beats a beautiful one you copied.

A whiteboard will not do the learning for you. What it does is make the effective methods the easy ones, because the blank board is always asking you to show what you know.

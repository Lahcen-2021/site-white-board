Maths is the subject where a whiteboard matters most. Students do not learn to solve an equation by reading the answer. They learn it by watching each step appear, one line under the other, with the reasoning spoken out loud. An online whiteboard lets you keep that rhythm in a classroom, in a remote lesson or in one-to-one tutoring.

This guide covers how to set up a board for maths, how to handle the most common topics and which habits make the biggest difference.

## Use a pen if you can

Writing "3x + 7 = 22" with a mouse is slow and messy. If you teach maths regularly, a pen input is worth it. Good options include:

- a tablet with a stylus (iPad with Apple Pencil, Android tablets with a pen, Samsung Galaxy Tab with S Pen)
- a laptop with a touch screen and pen support
- a small drawing tablet plugged into a computer by USB

Open SiteWhiteboard on the tablet, then share the tablet's screen in your video call or mirror it to the classroom projector. Your handwriting appears just as you write it.

No pen? A finger on a tablet or phone is still easier than a mouse for numbers. For typed work, the text tool (T) gives you clean, readable lines.

## Turn on the grid

A background grid makes maths neater. Fractions line up, columns of numbers stay straight and graphs look like graphs. In SiteWhiteboard, open the menu and switch on the grid. Turn it off again when you want a plain board for diagrams.

## Equations: one step per line

The biggest rule in written maths is also the easiest to forget on a big board: one step per line, and every line directly under the previous one.

1. Write the equation at the top in your main colour.
2. Write the operation you will apply on the right, in a second colour. For example "−7 both sides."
3. Write the result on the next line.
4. Repeat until x is alone, then put a box around the answer with the rectangle tool.

Using a separate colour for the operation makes a huge difference. Students who are lost can look back up the board and see exactly what happened between two lines.

## Fractions

Fractions are easier to see than to explain. Use the rectangle tool to draw a bar, then divide it with straight lines into equal parts. Colour in the parts that make up the fraction with a marker.

To compare 2/3 and 3/4, draw two bars of the same length, one under the other. Students immediately see which is longer. For adding fractions with different denominators, draw both bars, then cut them both into twelfths. The common denominator stops being a rule to memorise and becomes something they can see.

## Geometry

The shape tool has rectangles, ellipses, triangles and diamonds. They are a good start for geometry, but the real value is in what you add on top:

- label vertices with the text tool
- mark equal sides with small strokes using the pen
- draw angles with the pen and write the angle size next to them
- use the line tool for heights, diagonals and radii

Because shapes can be selected and moved, you can show that two triangles are congruent by dragging one on top of the other. Duplicate a shape with Ctrl+D, then move the copy to show a translation.

## Number lines and negative numbers

Draw a long horizontal line with the line tool and add tick marks with the pen. Write the numbers under the ticks. Now use arrows to show operations: an arrow from 3 that jumps 5 steps to the left lands on −2. For subtraction of negative numbers, two arrows in different colours make the "minus a minus" idea far less mysterious.

## Graphs and functions

With the grid on, draw the axes with the line tool and label them. Plot points with the pen, then join them. Sketching three versions of a function on the same axes, each in its own colour, shows how changing a parameter moves the graph. For example, y = x², y = x² + 2 and y = (x − 1)² side by side.

SiteWhiteboard is a drawing board, not a graphing calculator. For exact plots, draw the graph in a dedicated tool, take a screenshot and paste it onto the board with Ctrl+V. You can then annotate it by hand.

## Worked examples and practice

A good maths lesson moves from "watch me" to "do it with me" to "do it yourself." The board can follow the same path.

- **Watch me:** solve one example fully in the centre of the board.
- **Do it with me:** write a second, similar problem and ask students for each step before you write it.
- **Do it yourself:** write three problems in a corner. Students solve them on paper, then one student explains their answer while you write it.

Keep each example in its own area of the board. Students can always scroll back to the first example while working on the third.

## Tutoring one student

In one-to-one tutoring the board becomes a shared notebook. Ask the student to describe each step while you write. When they make a mistake, do not erase it. Circle it in red and write the correction next to it. Mistakes left visible teach more than a clean board.

At the end of the session, export the board as a PNG and send it to the student or their parents. They get a full record of what you covered, and you get a quick reminder before the next lesson.

## Quick checklist

- Grid on for arithmetic, algebra and graphs
- One step per line, operations in a second colour
- Shapes for fractions and geometry, lines and arrows for number lines
- Paste exact graphs as images when precision matters
- Export the board after every lesson

The tools are simple on purpose. A maths board does not need dozens of features. It needs clear handwriting, enough space and a way to keep what you wrote, which is exactly what a good online whiteboard gives you.

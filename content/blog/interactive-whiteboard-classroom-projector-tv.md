Interactive whiteboards and large touch displays can transform a classroom, but they are expensive, and many schools do not have one in every room. The good news is that most of the benefits come from the software, not the hardware. With a free online whiteboard and a screen that already exists in the room, you can get very close.

This guide explains several setups, from the simplest to the most interactive, and how to teach with each one.

## What makes a whiteboard "interactive"?

An interactive whiteboard lets you write and draw on a large screen that everyone can see, then save, move and reuse what you drew. Three parts make that happen:

1. **A big display** the whole class can see: a projector, a TV or a touch screen.
2. **An input device** to draw with: a touch screen, a tablet, a pen or a mouse.
3. **Whiteboard software** to draw in.

If your room already has a display, you only need to sort out the input and the software. The software can be a free online whiteboard such as SiteWhiteboard, which runs in any browser and needs no installation.

## Setup 1: Laptop and projector

**What you need:** a laptop connected to the projector or TV with an HDMI cable.

Open the whiteboard in the laptop's browser and make it full screen (F11 on most Windows browsers). Draw with the mouse or the trackpad.

This is the simplest setup and it works everywhere. Drawing with a mouse is slower than with a pen, so lean on the shape tool, the arrow tool and typed text, and keep freehand drawing for quick marks and circles.

## Setup 2: Tablet mirrored to the screen

**What you need:** a tablet (iPad, Android or Windows), and a way to put its screen on the projector or TV. That can be an adapter cable, a casting device, or screen mirroring built into the TV.

Open the whiteboard on the tablet and draw with your finger or a stylus. The class sees everything on the big screen.

This is the setup closest to a real smart board, and in one way it is better: you can walk around the room with the tablet, or hand it to a student at their desk. Nobody has to come to the front to write.

## Setup 3: Laptop with a drawing tablet

**What you need:** a laptop connected to the projector, plus a USB drawing tablet.

Drawing tablets are cheap and give you pen input on any computer. You write on the tablet on your desk and the strokes appear on the projected board. It takes a few minutes to get used to looking at the screen while your hand is on the desk, but most teachers adjust quickly.

## Setup 4: An existing interactive display

**What you need:** an interactive display or smart board that has a web browser.

Many interactive displays come with their own software, but you can also open an online whiteboard in the display's browser. SiteWhiteboard supports touch and pen, so students can draw directly on the screen with a finger. This is useful if you want the same tool in the classroom and at home, or if the built-in software is clumsy.

## Teaching with the board

Once the board is on the big screen, a few habits help the whole class follow.

**Write big.** What looks large on your tablet may be small on a projector at the back of the room. Zoom in on the area you are writing in, and choose the middle or thick marker size.

**Use high-contrast colours.** Projectors wash out pale colours. Dark blue, black and red show best. Test in your room before the lesson, especially with the lights on.

**Prepare before class.** Paste in diagrams, worksheets or photos before the lesson so you do not spend class time searching for files. You can also prepare a board template, save it as a project file and insert it into a fresh board each lesson.

**Move instead of erase.** Pan the board to a new space when you need room. The earlier work is still there if a student asks a question about it.

**Show the whole board at the end.** Press the fit button to zoom out and show how everything connects. It is a natural way to summarise.

## Getting students to the board

The best part of an interactive board is students using it.

- Pass the tablet to a student to solve a step, label a diagram or circle a mistake.
- Let groups present their ideas by adding sticky notes to the board.
- For quiz-style revision, show a diagram with labels missing and let students fill them in.

Each student's work stays on the board until you decide to clear it, and you can undo any accidental stroke.

## After the lesson

Export the board as a PNG and share it with the class on your learning platform or by printing it. Students who were absent get the full lesson notes, and everyone has a reference for homework.

Boards are saved in the browser of the device you used. If several teachers share one classroom computer, or if the computer clears its data overnight, export your boards before you leave.

## Is this as good as a real smart board?

Not in every way. A dedicated interactive display has a large touch surface, and that is great when students come to the front. But for explaining, annotating and sharing, a free online whiteboard on a projector or TV covers most of what teachers actually use. And the mirrored tablet setup gives you something a wall-mounted board cannot: the freedom to walk around the room.

Start with what is already in your classroom. Connect, open a board and teach one lesson with it. You will soon see which setup suits your room and your teaching.

Sticky notes are the most useful thing on any whiteboard. One idea per note, small enough to move, quick enough to write. Two of the best ways to use them are the team retrospective and affinity mapping. Both turn a messy pile of opinions into a few clear points the group can act on.

This guide explains how to run each one on a digital whiteboard, with timings you can copy.

## Why sticky notes work

A sticky note forces brevity. You cannot write a paragraph on one, so people boil their thought down to its core. Notes are also movable, which separates two jobs that groups often mix up: producing ideas and organising them. First everyone writes, then the group sorts.

On an online whiteboard, sticky notes have extra advantages. They never fall off the wall, handwriting is always readable when typed, and the board can be saved and exported. On SiteWhiteboard, double-tap an empty spot (or press N) to create a note, type, and drag it into place. The text shrinks to fit the note.

## Part 1: The team retrospective

A retrospective is a meeting at the end of a project or a work cycle where the team looks back and decides what to change. It is a standard part of agile methods like Scrum, but any team can use it.

### The board

Draw three columns with the rectangle tool and label them:

- **Went well**
- **Did not go well**
- **To try next time**

Use a different colour of note for each column if you like. Some teams prefer "Start, Stop, Continue" or "Glad, Sad, Mad." The format matters less than doing it regularly.

### Running it (45 minutes)

1. **Set the scene (5 minutes).** Remind everyone of the period you are looking back on and the rule: we discuss the work, not people.
2. **Silent writing (10 minutes).** Everyone writes notes for the first two columns, one point per note. On a shared screen with one facilitator, people can send their points in the meeting chat and the facilitator types them onto notes.
3. **Read and group (10 minutes).** Go through the notes. Move duplicates on top of each other and cluster similar points.
4. **Vote (5 minutes).** Each person gets three votes. The facilitator draws a dot on a note for each vote. Dots in a bright colour stand out.
5. **Discuss the top items (10 minutes).** Talk about the two or three notes with the most votes. Why did it happen?
6. **Agree on actions (5 minutes).** Write one or two concrete actions in "To try next time," each with an owner's name.

Export the board as a PNG and post it in the team channel. Start the next retrospective by checking whether last time's actions happened.

### Tips for retrospectives

- Keep actions small. "Write the release checklist before Friday" beats "improve communication."
- Rotate the facilitator so the same person does not always run it.
- If the same problem keeps appearing, that is the topic of the next retrospective.

## Part 2: Affinity mapping

Affinity mapping (also called an affinity diagram or the KJ method) is a way to find patterns in a large number of ideas or observations. Researchers use it after interviews, product teams after collecting feedback, and teachers after asking a class an open question.

### Step 1: One observation per note

Put each idea, quote or observation on its own sticky note. If you are working from interview notes or survey answers, this can mean fifty or a hundred notes. Do not filter yet.

### Step 2: Spread them out

Scatter all the notes across a big area of the board. An infinite canvas helps here because a large set of notes needs room.

### Step 3: Group in silence

Move notes that feel related next to each other. Do not name the groups yet and do not discuss. If a note fits two groups, make a copy (Ctrl+D) and put one in each.

Silent grouping matters. When groups are named too early, people force notes into existing categories instead of letting new patterns appear.

### Step 4: Name the groups

When the groups settle, write a short label above each one, in a different colour or with the text tool. A good label is a sentence that says something: "People don't trust the price until they see the total" is more useful than "Pricing."

### Step 5: Look for the bigger picture

Arrange the groups so related ones sit next to each other, and draw arrows between groups that influence each other. Often a few groups turn out to be symptoms of one bigger issue.

### Step 6: Decide what it means

Finish by writing the three most important findings on large notes at the top of the board. These are what you take into the next meeting.

## Doing this with a remote team

SiteWhiteboard does not have live multi-user editing, so for remote sessions one person facilitates and shares their screen. This works well in practice:

- participants write their notes in the meeting chat or a shared document
- the facilitator copies them onto sticky notes on the board
- the group tells the facilitator where to move notes while watching the screen

It is slower than everyone dragging notes at once, but it keeps one clear view and avoids the chaos of ten cursors. If your team needs everyone editing at the same time, use a collaborative whiteboard for that session.

## Small details that help

- Keep note colours meaningful: one colour per column, or per type of feedback.
- Use the fit button to show the whole board before discussing groups.
- Do not throw away the raw notes. Export the board before you tidy it.

Sticky notes look like a small tool, but they change how a group thinks. Give everyone a quiet moment to write, then let the patterns show themselves.

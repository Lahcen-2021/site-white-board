A mind map is a diagram that starts with one idea in the middle and spreads outwards into branches. Each branch is a subtopic, and each subtopic splits into smaller details. The result looks a bit like a tree seen from above, or a city map with roads leading out of the centre.

Mind maps are useful because they work the way people think about a topic: in connections, not in lists. This guide shows how to make one on an online whiteboard, step by step, and how to make it actually useful for studying, writing or planning.

## Why make a mind map online?

Paper works, but it has two problems. You run out of space at the edge of the page, and you cannot move a branch once it is drawn. On an online whiteboard the board is endless, and sticky notes and shapes can be dragged anywhere. When a branch grows bigger than you expected, you simply move things to make room.

You also keep the map. Boards on SiteWhiteboard are saved in your browser as you work, and you can export the finished map as a PNG to print, share or put in your notes.

## Step 1: Put the central idea in the middle

Open a board and put your main topic in the centre. Use an ellipse from the shape tool (S) and write the topic inside, or simply a big sticky note. Keep it short: "Photosynthesis," "Marketing plan," "World War I causes," "My essay."

Make the centre bigger than everything else. It anchors the whole map.

## Step 2: Add the main branches

Think of the four to seven big themes inside your topic. These are your main branches. For "Photosynthesis," they might be:

- inputs
- outputs
- light reactions
- the Calvin cycle
- where it happens

Put each theme on its own sticky note around the centre, spaced out like the numbers on a clock. Connect each one to the centre with an arrow (L). In SiteWhiteboard, arrows attach to shapes and notes, so they follow when you drag a note to a new spot.

## Step 3: Give each branch its own colour

Pick a different colour for each main branch and use it for everything that grows from that branch. Colour lets your brain group information at a glance. When you look at the map later, "everything green is about the Calvin cycle" is easier to remember than one big black diagram.

## Step 4: Add details with keywords, not sentences

Now grow each branch. Add smaller notes or handwritten words for the details, connected by lines. Use single words or short phrases: "chlorophyll," "thylakoid," "CO2 in," "glucose out."

Keywords are the most important rule of mind mapping. Full sentences turn a map back into notes and lose the overview. If you need more detail, the keyword is a reminder to look it up.

## Step 5: Draw connections across branches

The best mind maps show links between branches, not just from the centre outwards. If "ATP" appears in the light reactions and is used in the Calvin cycle, draw a dashed or coloured arrow between them. These cross-links are often what exams and real projects are really about.

## Step 6: Add small drawings

A quick sketch next to a keyword, such as a sun, a leaf or a simple chart, makes the map easier to remember. You do not need to draw well. A recognisable doodle is enough. You can also paste images with Ctrl+V or drag them onto the board.

## Step 7: Review and export

Zoom out with the fit button and look at the whole map. Is anything missing? Is one branch much bigger than the rest? Maybe it should be split into two.

When you are happy, export the map as a PNG. Print it, stick it on a wall, or add it to your digital notes.

## Mind map examples

**For studying a chapter:** chapter title in the centre, one branch per section heading, details from memory first, then checked against the book.

**For writing an essay:** your question in the centre, one branch per argument, evidence and examples on the smaller branches. The branches often become your paragraphs.

**For planning a project:** project name in the centre, branches for goals, tasks, people, budget, risks and dates.

**For making a decision:** the decision in the centre, one branch per option, with pros in green and cons in red under each.

## Mind map vs other diagrams

A mind map is not the right tool for everything:

- for a sequence of steps, a [flowchart](/blog/how-to-draw-a-flowchart-online/) is clearer
- for grouping many separate ideas from a team, sticky notes and affinity grouping work better
- for comparing options on two criteria, a 2x2 matrix is faster

Use a mind map when one topic has many connected parts and you want to see all of them at once.

## Common mistakes

- **Too much text.** Keywords only.
- **Too many main branches.** More than seven gets hard to follow. Group some together.
- **All one colour.** You lose the main advantage of the format.
- **Copying, not recalling.** For study, draw the map from memory first. Copying from notes is much less effective.

Start with a topic you know well to get used to the format, then use it on something you are learning. A good mind map takes 15 to 30 minutes and saves hours of rereading.

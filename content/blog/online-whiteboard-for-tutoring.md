Online tutoring has one big challenge compared with sitting at the same table: you cannot just grab a pen and draw on the student's notebook. A whiteboard on a shared screen fills that gap. It becomes the shared piece of paper where you explain, the student tries, and both of you can see the thinking.

This guide covers the equipment, the setup and the lesson habits that make a whiteboard work in one-to-one tutoring.

## The equipment

You do not need much, but a few choices make a big difference.

**A pen input device.** This is the most important upgrade for a tutor. Writing with a mouse is slow and hard to read, especially for maths, science and languages with special characters. Options:

- a tablet with a stylus, such as an iPad with Apple Pencil or an Android tablet with a pen
- a laptop with a touch screen and pen support
- a USB drawing tablet, which is the cheapest option and works with any computer

**A stable internet connection.** Screen sharing with live drawing needs a steady connection more than a fast one. Use a cable if your Wi-Fi drops.

**A headset.** Students hear you clearly, and you avoid echo while you talk and draw.

## The setup

Here is a setup that works for most tutors:

1. Start the video call on your computer as usual (Zoom, Google Meet, Microsoft Teams or any other app with screen sharing).
2. Open SiteWhiteboard in a separate browser window. If you draw on a tablet, open it on the tablet and join the call from the tablet too, or mirror the tablet to your computer.
3. Share only the whiteboard window, not your whole screen.
4. Keep a second window with the lesson material, such as a PDF or exercise sheet, so you can copy questions onto the board.

Because SiteWhiteboard needs no account, the student does not have to sign up for anything. This matters for younger students and for families who do not want another login.

## Prepare one board per student

Before the lesson, open a board for the student and prepare it:

- the date and the topic at the top
- screenshots of the exercises you will work on, pasted with Ctrl+V
- a sticky note with the goal of the lesson

You can keep a board going across several lessons and pick up where you left off, because the board is saved in your browser. For many students, save each board as a project file from the boards menu. Before the next lesson, open a fresh board and insert the file to continue. The free plan holds three boards at a time, and Pro removes the limit.

## A one-hour lesson structure

**Warm-up (5 minutes).** Write one quick question from last lesson on the board. The student answers out loud and you write their answer. This shows you what stuck.

**New material (15 minutes).** Explain the new topic on the board, step by step. Ask the student questions as you go rather than talking for fifteen minutes straight.

**Guided practice (20 minutes).** Paste or write a problem. The student tells you each step and you write it, or the student writes if they have a pen device. When they make a mistake, circle it in red and let them find the fix.

**Independent practice (10 minutes).** The student solves one problem on paper with the camera on, then explains their answer while you write it on the board.

**Wrap-up (10 minutes).** Write a three-line summary of the lesson together. Add the homework on a sticky note.

## Habits that make tutoring boards better

**Let the student lead the pen.** Even when you hold the pen, ask "what do I write next?" A board that is built from the student's words teaches them more than a perfect explanation.

**Keep mistakes visible.** Do not erase errors. Correct them next to the original in a second colour. Students learn from seeing what went wrong.

**Use colour consistently.** One colour for your explanations, another for the student's answers, red for corrections, green for the final answer.

**Zoom to the problem.** Students often watch on a laptop or even a phone. Zoom in so the work fills their screen.

**Use space, not erasing.** When you need room, move to an empty area of the board instead of wiping. The whole lesson stays on one board.

## Sharing notes with students and parents

At the end of each lesson, export the board as a PNG and send it to the student, or to the parents if the student is young. This gives them:

- a full record of the lesson to review before homework
- proof of progress over weeks of lessons
- a clear picture for parents of what their child is working on

Many tutors say this one habit improves how families see the value of their lessons. Parents rarely sit in on the lesson, but they do look at a clear summary.

## What a whiteboard cannot do

SiteWhiteboard has no live collaboration, so the student cannot draw on your board from their own device at the same time. For most tutoring this is fine, because you control the board and the student talks you through their thinking. If you want the student to draw directly, ask them to open their own board and share their screen for a few minutes. You can switch back when they are done.

Good tutoring is mostly about listening to how a student thinks. A whiteboard makes that thinking visible, for both of you.
